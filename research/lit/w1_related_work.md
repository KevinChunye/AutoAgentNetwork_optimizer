Date: 2026-10-27. Author: lit-scout (session W1). Feeds the related-work section of the working paper "Depth before width: shared failure and the price of verification in multi-LLM systems" and `research/plan.md` (Plan v4: width, depth and verification). No API budget was used; web reading only.

## How to read this note

- Every statement below comes from a page I opened on 2026-10-27: the arXiv abstract page (`arxiv.org/abs/<id>`) and the arXiv HTML version (`arxiv.org/html/<id>`) of each paper. I have no shell here, so I could not download PDFs; "HTML" means the HTML text as processed by `WebFetch`.
- `WebFetch` returns a digest written by a small model, not raw text. It refuses to reproduce more than about 125 characters in one quote, so no abstract below is verbatim. Where a sentence is in quotation marks it is a short string the digest reported; where it is not, it is my paraphrase of the digest. Where two digests of the same page overlapped I compared them and note any disagreement.
- Read depth per paper is in the table. "Abstract only" marks anything I could read only from the abstract page.
- Labels as in the other lit notes: **[inference]** my reasoning, not a sourced claim; **UNVERIFIED (reason)** I tried and could not confirm.
- Plain definitions used below. *Width*: more agents or more samples at the same setting (votes, ensembles). *Depth*: more reasoning per call, or a stronger tier. *Verification*: choosing among answers with a judge, a reward model or a label-free rule. *All-wrong rate (beta)*: the share of items on which every system in a pool is wrong.

### Brief versus reality

- All 11 IDs resolve to a paper on the topic named in the brief. No ID points to a different paper.
- Two names in the brief are method or benchmark names, not titles: **LLMSelector** (title: "Optimizing Model Selection for Compound AI Systems") and **ACBench** (title: "Can Compressed LLMs Truly Act? ...").
- **ACBench does not belong in the related-work section for this thesis.** "Pruning" there means weight pruning (SparseGPT, Wanda, magnitude) and "compression" means GPTQ, AWQ and SmoothQuant on single models. The paper says nothing about multi-agent systems or agent counts (digest of the HTML).
- The title of 2602.08003 says neither "error floor" nor "greedy mutual information"; both are inside the paper.
- For 2603.06612 the arXiv page prints v1 as 20 Feb 2026 although the ID prefix is 2603; I report the printed date.

### Findings that contradict or qualify the thesis

No paper contradicts the three core claims outright. Four findings qualify them and should be stated in the paper, not ignored:

1. **2606.27288** (a) already states and certifies the width cap for any select-one policy, so the ceiling is not our contribution; (b) reports that a tetrachoric-calibrated single-factor model "still underprices the all-wrong tail" by about 2.5 times on open-ended mathematics (its appendix gives k = 17 all-wrong items out of 330 for MATH-500 with 67 models; that these are the same k = 17 is my inference, the digest did not tie them), which qualifies "failures are dominated by one shared difficulty axis" in the extreme tail; (c) reports that the same GPQA-Diamond questions asked free-response instead of multiple-choice have all-wrong rate 0.127 versus about 0, so the difficulty axis depends on answer format.
2. **2504.01005**: generative verification does overtake self-consistency at high budgets (for example +3.8% at 128 times the compute on MATH with Llama-3.1-8B, and almost double SC on AIME24 at 256 times). "Verification loses" is true at practical budgets, not in general.
3. **2502.14815**: choosing a different vendor's model per module gained 5 to 70 accuracy points over the best single model on structured pipelines (percentage points in the four rows I could check by subtraction). That is role allocation under a labelled search, not parallel width, but it shows that mixing models is not always capped.
4. **2511.07396**: reports a label-free agreement cascade at 62.5% for $0.0019 per question against 57% for self-consistency with the strongest model at $0.0053 on MATH-500. I could not tell from the digest which cascade this is (see its entry).

## 1. When Does Combining Language Models Help? A Co-Failure Ceiling on Routing, Voting, and Mixture-of-Agents Across 67 Frontier Models (arXiv preprint, 2026)

- Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.27288 ; HTML https://arxiv.org/html/2606.27288
- Authors: Josef Chen (sole author). v1: 25 June 2026. Venue: none shown on the abstract page. Read depth: abstract page plus the whole HTML (two WebFetch parts, 0 to 100,000 and 100,000 to 163,252 characters).
- What it does: argues that routing, voting, cascades, fusion and mixture-of-agents are capped by the rate beta at which every model in the pool fails the same query, and that average pairwise error correlation cannot measure this. Proposes a Clopper-Pearson lower bound on beta from one graded held-out set as a "$0 certificate" for the gain a router could reach, and tests it on 67 models from 21 providers (digest: GPT-5.5, Claude Opus 4.8, Gemini 3.1 Pro, Grok-4.3, DeepSeek V4, Qwen3.7-Max, Kimi K2.7 among them).
- Key numbers (quoted or read):
  - Prop. 1 (Section 5): any router, vote or cascade that outputs one member's answer has accuracy at most 1 minus beta; the gain over the single best equals Pr[single best wrong] minus beta. Abstract: "accuracy cannot exceed 1−β, where β is the rate at which every model is wrong on the same query".
  - Abstract: "A Clopper–Pearson bound on β turns one graded, held-out query set into a $0 certificate".
  - Prop. 2 and 3: under a common-shock error model the single-factor Gaussian-copula estimate of beta from the pairwise correlation shrinks toward zero as the pool grows while the true beta stays positive; for m of at least 3, beta is not determined by pairwise statistics. Abstract: "a correctly (tetrachoric) calibrated single-factor model still underprices the all-wrong tail".
  - Underpricing: "about 2.5 times on open-ended mathematics (90% CI 1.7 to 3.4, k=17)". Appendix C (digest): beta = 0.052 with k = 17 all-wrong items of n = 330 MATH-500 items answered by all 67 models. **[inference]** The k = 17 in the two places is the same count; the digest did not say so. Appendix F (digest): Pearson correlation of the 0/1 error indicators 0.27 against tetrachoric 0.51; Appendix A.2: "the Pearson coefficient of 0/1 indicators biases ρ (hence λ) downward".
  - Headline: "combining models rarely beats the single best model without a strong query-level routing signal". Oracle gain 0.044 (CI 0.027 to 0.062) on the saturated mix and 0.120 (CI 0.075 to 0.155) on MMLU-Pro. A learned router captures about 9% of the gain on the mix with an interval that spans zero; an LLM used as router sends 100% of queries to the single best model and captures none (Section 5). Unweighted majority voting has mean gain -0.10 on hard sets and -0.02 on saturated sets (digest wording: "over the best member"; I could not tell what the pool of voters is).
  - Fusion: at matched quality, low-correlation heterogeneous fusion beats Self-MoA by +0.027 on average, which the paper calls "supported in one regime, not established".
  - Answer format: re-asking GPQA-Diamond in free-response form gives beta = 0.127 (k = 10 of 79 questions, Clopper-Pearson interval 0.062 to 0.220) against about 0 for multiple choice; mean accuracy falls from 0.66 to 0.51 and best-model accuracy from 0.91 to 0.77 (Section 5).
  - Pools: pillar experiments 15 models across 9 families on GSM8K, MMLU, ARC-Challenge, MATH-500 and MMLU-Pro with 100 to 200 queries each; market-scale sets MATH-500 (n = 330, 67 models), MATH-Hard (n = 298), code_contests (63 problems, 18 models), GPQA-Diamond (52 models, 130 queries).
  - Reasoning and cost: Appendix D, "pure reasoning/'thinking' variants are excluded" so programmatic grading stays clean; reasoning models that spent their budget on hidden reasoning and returned nothing were re-queried at larger budgets (Appendix G). The paper does not analyse thinking budgets or depth versus width. Spend is reported as about $270 for the experiments and about $560 account usage in total, metered through the OpenRouter usage endpoint; Prop. 4 prices routing with a single shadow price of the inference dollar.
  - Judges: grading is programmatic except open-ended GPQA, graded by a five-judge LLM panel (pairwise kappa 0.73 to 0.92). Dawid-Skene is not mentioned in the second half of the HTML (the first-half digest did not mention it either).
  - Citations: cites Li et al. 2024 "More agents is all you need" (arXiv 2402.05120; not opened by me) and, for the copula floor, Turkmen et al. 2026 (our entry 9); Appendix A.2 says "We claim none of these".
- Quote check: the brief's line "Gains come from models failing on different questions, not from adding more models" came from a digest of the abstract page, not a verified exact string. A second pass on the HTML found no such exact sentence; the closest is the abstract's last sentence, paraphrased as "the gains come from models that fail on different questions rather than from adding more of them". **Do not put quotation marks around it in the paper**; paraphrase.
- Relevance to AutoAgentNetwork: **threatens novelty** (the ceiling, the certificate, the router result) and **supports** the width and verification claims. It does not touch depth: its pool excludes pure thinking variants and it never holds dollars fixed while moving from width to reasoning effort. It also **qualifies** one assumption: a single shared difficulty factor under-predicts the all-wrong tail.
- Draft related-work sentence: "Chen (2026) shows that any routing, voting or cascade policy that returns one model's answer is bounded by one minus the share of queries on which all models fail, gives a Clopper-Pearson certificate for that share, and reports that an LLM used as router captured none of the available gain across a pool of 67 models. We take that ceiling as given and ask the question it leaves open, namely how the ceiling itself moves when one system reasons longer, and at what price."
- Resources: the arXiv page shows CC BY 4.0 for the paper. Appendix C says outcome matrices and scripts (`beta_certificate.py`, `realizability.py`, `cascade.py`) are released; no repository URL appeared in the text I read: **UNVERIFIED (URL not seen; data and code licence not stated)**.
- Open questions it raises: does beta fall when the same models run with reasoning on (the paper leaves thinking variants out)? Does a free-per-item-difficulty model, as in the Atlas, reproduce held-out beta where the single-factor copula does not (see "So what")?

## 2. Agent psychometrics: Task-level performance prediction in agentic coding benchmarks (arXiv preprint, 2026)

- Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00594 ; HTML https://arxiv.org/html/2604.00594
- Authors: Chris Ge, Daria Kryvosheieva, Daniel Fried, Uzay Girit, Kaivalya Hariharan. v1: 1 April 2026 (v2 revised 20 July 2026). Venue: none shown. Read depth: abstract page plus the whole HTML (0 to 100,000 and 100,000 to 171,038 characters).
- What it does: augments Item Response Theory with task features (embeddings of the task and gold solution, rubric scores from an LLM judge, repository-state features from an auditor agent) to predict whether an agent solves a given task, and splits agent ability into an LLM part and a scaffold part so that leaderboards can be pooled.
- Key numbers (quoted or read):
  - Model: Rasch, P(y=1) = sigmoid(theta_i minus beta_j) with agent ability theta and task difficulty beta (Section 2.1.2); theta = theta_LLM + theta_scaffold (Eq. 4). "we decompose an agent's IRT ability into the abilities of its underlying LLM and scaffold." (Section 3.2.2). Section 6: "agent ability decomposes additively into independent LLM and scaffold abilities, without pairwise interaction terms."
  - Data: SWE-bench Verified, SWE-bench Pro, Terminal-Bench 2.0 (majority outcome over attempts) and GSO, from "publicly available agent evaluation data" with per-dataset sources in the GitHub repo (Section 2.1.1). Appendix G.3 reports 38 unique LLMs and 112 LLM-scaffold combinations in the response set analysed there; the main text gives no per-benchmark counts.
  - New tasks (AUC-ROC, Table 2): combined features 0.841 (SWE-bench Verified), 0.763 (Pro), 0.787 (GSO), 0.792 (Terminal-Bench 2.0); baseline 0.656 to 0.732; oracle 0.916 to 0.945.
  - New agents (Table 4): 0.930 on SWE-bench Verified and 0.923 on Terminal-Bench 2.0 against baselines 0.852 and 0.839 (held-out agents must have LLM and scaffold seen separately in training).
  - New benchmarks (Table 5): 0.674 (SWE-bench Pro) and 0.719 (GSO); "decent generalization to held-out benchmarks, beating the baseline but still far from the oracle" (Section 5.3).
  - Dimensionality: the model is one-dimensional; the authors note agents "can fail on different tasks in the same benchmark for different reasons" (Introduction) but I found no test of whether one dimension is enough (digest of both halves).
  - Cost, reasoning effort: none discussed.
- Relevance to AutoAgentNetwork: **threatens novelty on method** (a latent ability-minus-difficulty logit model fitted to SWE-bench Verified leaderboard data is already published) and **supports** the idea that one difficulty axis ranks held-out agents well (AUC 0.930). It **differs** in what it asks: it predicts the item from its text and never studies repeats, votes, selection, reasoning effort or dollars.
- Draft related-work sentence: "Ge et al. (2026) fit a Rasch model with additive LLM and scaffold abilities to SWE-bench Verified, SWE-bench Pro, Terminal-Bench 2.0 and GSO and predict unseen tasks from task text (AUC 0.76 to 0.84) and unseen agents (AUC 0.92 to 0.93); they assume a single ability and difficulty axis. We use the same logit scale but test what that shared difficulty implies for adding agents, adding reasoning and choosing among answers."
- Resources: code https://github.com/dariakryvosheieva/agent-psychometrics (named in the abstract footnote; not opened). Paper licence CC BY 4.0 per the arXiv page; no code or data licence stated in the text.
- Open questions it raises: their 112 combinations versus our 173 systems on SWE-bench Verified: do the two fits rank items the same way? Does the additive LLM-plus-scaffold form hold when the scaffold is a multi-agent network?

## 3. Are More LLM Calls All You Need? Towards Scaling Laws of Compound Inference Systems (arXiv preprint, 2024)

- Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.02419 ; HTML https://arxiv.org/html/2403.02419
- Authors: Lingjiao Chen, Jared Quincy Davis, Boris Hanin, Peter Bailis, Ion Stoica, Matei Zaharia, James Zou. v1: 4 March 2024. Venue: none shown on the arXiv page (I did not fill one in from memory). Read depth: abstract page plus the whole HTML.
- What it does: studies two designs built on repeated calls of one model: Vote (majority over K answers) and Filter-Vote (an LM keeps only answers it judges correct, then majority). Shows that accuracy can first rise and then fall as K grows and attributes this to a mixture of easy and hard queries; derives an analytical model that predicts the best K from a small sample.
- Key numbers (quoted or read):
  - Abstract: "the performance of both Vote and Filter-Vote can first increase but then decrease as a function of the number of LM calls"; "more LM calls lead to higher performance on “easy” queries, but lower performance on “hard” queries."
  - Difficulty indicator (Lemma 1): d_V(x) = max over wrong answers a of Pr[answer = a] minus Pr[answer = correct]; positive means hard. Two-level dataset (easy fraction alpha, easy accuracy p1 above 1/2, hard accuracy p2 below 1/2): Theorem 2 gives four curve shapes; Theorem 4 gives the optimal K* as a closed form.
  - Setup: GPT-3.5-turbo-0125 only, temperature 0.1, 400 samples per question, averaged over 1,000 runs; MMLU Physics (151 questions), TruthfulQA (817), GPQA Diamond (198), AVeriTeC (500). The model is fitted on K = 1 to 5 and predicts up to K = 100, with mean squared error about 1e-6 to 1e-4 (synthetic difficulty settings).
  - Filter: Section 5 says "We use GPT-3.5-turbo-0125 on the real-world datasets"; the filter prompt (Appendix D.2) shows the question and a candidate answer and asks the model to find problems with it; no labels are used except for scoring. The text does not state outright that the filter is the same model as the generator (digest). One digest of Section 5 gave the easy/hard sentence for both systems in the opposite direction to the abstract; I could not settle whether that is a typo in the paper or a digest error, so cite the abstract's direction only.
  - Cost: "we do not discuss the cost of LM calls; this is an important dimension to weigh in practice" (Section 6). Limits (Appendix B): two system types, "relatively objective language tasks", one model.
  - Datasets' licences (Appendix D.1): GPQA MIT, AVeriTeC CC BY-NC 4.0, TruthfulQA and MMLU Apache 2.0.
- Relevance to AutoAgentNetwork: **supports** (heterogeneous item difficulty is what bends the width curve) and **differs**: one model's repeats, calls instead of dollars, no cross-system set, no reasoning axis. It also lists "predicting query difficulty without querying the LMs" as open (Appendix B).
- Draft related-work sentence: "Chen et al. (2024) show for GPT-3.5-turbo that majority vote and filter-then-vote can lose accuracy as the number of calls grows, because more calls help easy queries and hurt hard ones, and derive the optimal number of calls from a two-level difficulty model; they count calls, not dollars, and study one model's repeats. We fit the difficulty structure across hundreds of systems and compare width with reasoning effort at measured cost."
- Resources: the paper says "We will release the code and datasets used in this paper"; no repository link in the text I read.
- Open questions it raises: their K* needs p1, p2 and alpha; can a cross-system factor model supply them without sampling the target system?

## 4. When To Solve, When To Verify: Compute-Optimal Problem Solving and Generative Verification for LLM Reasoning (COLM 2025)

- Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.01005 ; HTML https://arxiv.org/html/2504.01005
- Authors: Nishad Singhi, Hritik Bansal, Arian Hosseini, Aditya Grover, Kai-Wei Chang, Marcus Rohrbach, Anna Rohrbach. v1: 1 April 2025. Venue: "COLM 2025" (arXiv comment). Read depth: abstract page plus the whole HTML (0 to 100,000 and 100,000 to 122,481 characters; the second part held only appendices K.7, K.8 and L).
- What it does: with a fixed inference budget, compares Self-Consistency (SC: many solutions, majority vote) with Generative Reward Models (GenRM: fewer solutions, spend the rest on verification chains of thought), and fits scaling laws for the best split.
- Key numbers (quoted or read):
  - Abstract (as the digest gave it): "SC is more compute-efficient than GenRM for most practical inference budgets"; the abstract also says GenRM "first matches SC after consuming up to 8× the inference compute and requires significantly more compute to outperform it".
  - Compute is FLOPs from token counts, C(S,V) = S(1 + lambda V) with lambda the verification-to-solution token ratio. Tokens are the proxy; no dollars.
  - Llama-3.1-8B on MATH: GenRM-FT matches SC after up to 8 times the compute and needs 128 times to beat it, for a 3.8% gain. AIME24 (verifier trained on MATH): GenRM almost doubles SC's performance at 256 times the compute. Qwen-2.5-7B: SC peaks with 64 times less compute; GenRM-FT gains 5.4% at 512 times. QwQ-32B (a thinking model) on AIME 2025: GenRM needs 4 times to match and gains 2.5% at 16 times. Llama-3.3-70B GenRM-Base: SC peaks with 4 times less compute; GenRM gains 1.7% at 64 times. GPQA-Diamond: 256 times for +2.5%. LiveCodeBench: 16 times to match, 64 times for +3.5%.
  - Scaling laws (Llama-3.1-8B, GenRM-FT): S_opt proportional to C^0.57 and V_opt to C^0.39, so "the solutions should be scaled more rapidly than verifications". Subsets: 128 MATH problems and 64 GPQA problems.
  - Verifier strength: GenRM-FT reaches GenRM-Base performance with up to 16 times less compute (Section 5.1). Appendix F: a Llama 3 8B verifier on 70B solutions cuts the compute needed to match SC from 8 times to 4 times, but "it still does not outperform SC at low compute budgets". The main experiments are self-verification (the same model family solves and verifies); the GenRM-FT teacher is GPT-4o with access to ground-truth solutions.
- Relevance to AutoAgentNetwork: **supports** the claim that verification must beat the cheaper width alternative before it pays, and is the nearest compute-matched width-versus-verify comparison. It **differs**: FLOPs not dollars; math and a few other sets with trained or same-family verifiers; no cross-vendor judge; it never compares a judge with the judge model answering the question itself. I saw no per-item difficulty analysis in the parts read. It also **qualifies** the claim: verification does win at large budgets and with a thinking-model verifier (4 times to match).
- Draft related-work sentence: "Singhi et al. (2025) find that, at matched FLOPs, self-consistency beats generative verification at most practical budgets (verification needs up to 8 times the compute to match it) and that compute should go to solutions faster than to verifications (exponents 0.57 and 0.39); they use same-family verifiers and FLOPs. We ask the dollar-priced version with a cross-vendor judge and with the judge's own answer as the comparator."
- Resources: code https://github.com/nishadsinghi/sc-genrm-scaling (named in the abstract); footnote 1: "We will release the data, model, and code in the camera-ready version." Licence not checked.
- Open questions it raises: at matched dollars with cache pricing, where does the SC-versus-GenRM crossover sit? Does a stronger judge model answering by itself dominate a judge over cheap answers?

## 5. Optimizing Model Selection for Compound AI Systems (arXiv preprint, 2025; the method is called LLMSelector)

- Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.14815 ; HTML https://arxiv.org/html/2502.14815
- Authors: Lingjiao Chen, Jared Quincy Davis, Boris Hanin, Peter Bailis, Matei Zaharia, James Zou, Ion Stoica. v1: 20 February 2025. Venue: none shown (the digest says the HTML page does not state one either). Read depth: abstract page plus the whole HTML.
- What it does: for a compound system with several LLM modules (generator, critic, refiner, debaters, and so on), chooses which LLM runs each module. LLMSelector iteratively gives one module at a time the model with the best estimated module-wise performance, using an LLM "diagnoser" for the estimate, and stops when no module improves. It rests on two assumptions: end-to-end performance is roughly monotone in each module's performance, and an LLM can estimate module performance.
- Key numbers (quoted or read):
  - Abstract: "LLMSelector confers 5%-70% accuracy gains compared to using the same LLM for all modules." Table 2 gives absolute accuracies, so the range is in percentage points for most rows: TableArithmetic best single model 30% to 100%, TableBias 44% to 100%, LiveCodeBench 89% to 95%, FEVER 65% to 70%. Two rows do not match plain subtraction in the digest (SimpleQA 20% to 28% listed as +7%; CommonGenHard 75% to 84% listed as +11%): **UNVERIFIED (check against the authors' data before quoting those two)**.
  - Systems: self-refine (generator, critic, refiner), multi-agent debate (3 generators, 3 debaters), locate-solve. Ten models from several vendors (GPT-4o, GPT-4o mini, GPT-4-Turbo, Claude 3.5 Sonnet and Haiku, Gemini 1.5 Pro and Flash, Llama 3.1 405B and 70B, Qwen 2.5 72B). Datasets: LiveCodeBench, CommonGenHard, SimpleQA, FEVER, TableArithmetic, TableBias, with 100 to 4,326 items each and 50% used for training.
  - Labels: the search uses ground-truth answers on the training split (the diagnoser prompt says to "Assume that the desired answer is 100% correct"), so it is not label-free. API calls scale linearly with the number of modules; in one case study it tried 10 allocations against 25 for exhaustive search.
  - Monotonicity: the paper says its assumptions "are not always satisfied in practice" and gives no empirical test (Section 4.2).
  - Critic role, anecdotal: in one LiveCodeBench example GPT-4o as critic caught an error while "Claude 3.5 Sonnet as the critic tends to agree with its initial generation" (Figure 7 caption).
- Relevance to AutoAgentNetwork: **qualifies** the width cap (different models in different roles can beat any single model), **supports** the point that the choice of who judges matters, and is a **baseline** for per-role model choice. It does not address votes, shared difficulty or depth.
- Draft related-work sentence: "Chen et al. (2025) show that assigning different vendors' models to the modules of a fixed compound system can gain up to 70 accuracy points over using one model everywhere, using a labelled search with an LLM diagnoser; the choice is a per-role assignment in a fixed pipeline, and cost is counted in API calls. We study the complementary axes of how many agents, how much reasoning, and who selects."
- Resources: code https://github.com/LLMSELECTOR/LLMSELECTOR (footnote in Section 1; not opened); the paper says it releases intermediate LLM outputs. Licence of code and data not stated.
- Open questions it raises: is the large gain on TableArithmetic and TableBias a sign of structured tasks where one model is much better at one sub-step (a different difficulty axis per module)?

## 6. Can Compressed LLMs Truly Act? An Empirical Evaluation of Agentic Capabilities in LLM Compression (ICML 2025 poster; benchmark: ACBench)

- Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.19433 ; HTML https://arxiv.org/html/2505.19433
- Authors: Peijie Dong, Zhenheng Tang, Xiang Liu, Lujun Li, Xiaowen Chu, Bo Li. v1: 26 May 2025. Venue: "Accepted by ICML2025 as Poster" (arXiv comment). Read depth: abstract page plus the first 100,000 of 178,842 HTML characters (appendices not read).
- What it does: introduces the Agent Compression Benchmark, which tests how post-training compression (quantization: GPTQ, AWQ, SmoothQuant; pruning: SparseGPT, Wanda, magnitude) affects an LLM's agentic abilities in four capabilities: action execution (T-Eval), workflow generation (WorfBench), long-context understanding, and real-world application (AgentBoard tasks). It also proposes three analysis metrics (ERank, Top-K ranking consistency, Energy).

## 7. Conformal Cascade: Distribution-Free Accuracy Guarantees for Multi-Tier LLM Inference (arXiv preprint, 2026)

- Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.25018 ; HTML https://arxiv.org/html/2607.25018
- Authors: Yifan Dou, Shikan Lian, Shibo Li. v1: 27 July 2026. Venue: none shown. Read depth: abstract page plus the whole HTML (0 to 100,000 and 100,000 to 159,986 characters).
- What it does: a multi-tier cascade where a query is accepted at a tier when its conformal prediction set shrinks to one answer, and deferred otherwise. The nonconformity score is one minus the sampling frequency of an answer over N self-consistency samples (no log-probabilities needed); each tier is calibrated on a labelled split. Claims a distribution-free, finite-sample accuracy guarantee of 1 minus K times alpha for the accepting tier, tightened to 1 minus alpha under a "selection-preservation" assumption (Assumption 3), which the paper says "is not derivable from split CP alone" (Section 3.4).
- Key numbers (quoted or read):
  - 18 multiple-choice benchmarks (AGI-Eval, ARC-Challenge, Belebele-Eng, BoolQ, Cosmos-QA, GPQA-Diamond, HellaSwag, MathQA, MedMCQA, MedQA-USMLE-4, MMLU, MMLU-Pro, PIQA, PubmedQA, QASC, SciQ, Social-i-QA, TruthfulQA) and four two-tier open-weight families (Llama 3.2-3B to 3.1-8B; Gemma-4-E4B to Gemma-4-31B; Ministral-3-3B to 14B; Phi-3.5-mini to Phi-4).
  - "CC strictly improves on 49 pairs, ties within 0.05 pp on 6, and underperforms on 17" (Section 5.2) against the strongest calibration-tuned heuristic cascade; largest gain +4.6 points (Ministral on MathQA), largest loss -4.5 points (Phi on AGI-Eval). Tier-1 acceptance at alpha = 0.10 on Llama ranges from 0% (MMLU-Pro, GPQA-Diamond) to 95.6% (Belebele-Eng).
  - Cost is in relative per-call units: tier 1 costs 1 and tier 2 costs 2.7 for every family (Appendix B and J), "to decouple cross-family comparisons from provider-specific pricing"; with 80% acceptance the expected cost is 1.54, a 43% saving over always using the strong tier. No dollars, no closed APIs, compute on four B200 GPUs.
  - Limits: the guarantee "is marginal, not conditional on query difficulty" (Section 6); conditional coverage is not reported (Remark 6); seven cells in Table 10 exceed the alpha + 0.02 band (Appendix I). Appendix G: on MMLU, deferral on hard queries "coincides with queries where the strong model also fails", so selection cannot recover those answers. Extension to open-ended generation is left to future work (abstract).
- Relevance to AutoAgentNetwork: **baseline** for escalation by agreement (its signal is agreement among samples of the cheap model). It **supports** the point that no cascade can fix items both tiers fail (Appendix G), and it **differs** in needing labelled calibration data, in multiple-choice only, and in relative cost.
- Draft related-work sentence: "Dou et al. (2026) defer a query to a larger model unless a conformal set built from the small model's self-consistency frequencies has one element, with a marginal accuracy guarantee that needs labelled calibration data; on 18 multiple-choice benchmarks it beats tuned heuristic cascades on 49 of 72 family-benchmark pairs. Their cost is a fixed ratio of 2.7, not dollars, and their guarantee is not conditional on item difficulty."
- Resources: the digest says a Pareto visualisation "is released with the code" but no URL or licence appears in the text read: **UNVERIFIED**. Paper licence CC BY 4.0 per the arXiv page.
- Open questions it raises: how does its deferral rule behave on items where the cheap model is consistently wrong (high agreement, wrong)? The paper does not test this directly.

## 9. Don't Always Pick the Highest-Performing Model: An Information Theoretic View of LLM Ensemble Selection (arXiv preprint, 2026)

- Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08003 ; HTML https://arxiv.org/html/2602.08003 ; earlier notes in `phase4_sources.md` section 1 (read first; this entry re-checks them).
- Authors: Yigit Turkmen, Baturalp Buyukates, Melih Bastopcu. v1: 8 February 2026. Venue: none shown (no comment or journal reference on the abstract page). Read depth: abstract page plus the whole HTML (0 to 100,000 and 100,000 to 197,891 characters).
- What it does: budgeted selection of which LLMs to query, as maximising the mutual information between the true label and the chosen models' answers; a greedy algorithm estimates the terms from data. To explain why adding models stops helping, it models correlated errors with a Gaussian copula and proves a non-zero error floor.
- Key numbers (quoted or read), all consistent with `phase4_sources.md`:
  - Theorem 4.4 (MAP information saturation), uniform pairwise latent correlation rho above 0 and accuracy alpha above 1/2: the limit of the MAP error as the number of models m grows is Phi(Phi^-1(1 - alpha) / sqrt(rho)), which is above 0 (Eq. 11). The text before the theorem says "lower-bounded by" while the display is an equality; the appendix proof gives the equality.
  - Proof sketch (Appendix C): given the shared factor U the errors are independent Bernoulli with probability p(u); majority vote fails exactly when p(U) is above 1/2, so the floor is P(p(U) above 1/2). **[inference]** This is the plan's "vote ceiling" (share of items whose most common answer is right, one minus the floor) for a Gaussian latent; the thesis's version leaves the distribution of p(U) free.
  - Remark C.1: the floor lies in (0, 1/2) for any rho above 0, goes to 0 as rho goes to 0 and approaches 1 minus alpha as rho goes to 1.
  - Theorem D.1 ("price of difficulty", Appendix D): with a hidden prompt difficulty D independent of the label and answers conditionally independent given (label, D), I(Y; X_S) = I(Y; X_S | D) minus I(Y; D | X_S); the first term is monotone submodular in the chosen set.
  - Experiments: MEDMCQA (12 LLMs, 4,183 validation questions turned into 8,366 binary samples; best Greedy MI error 16.3% at k = 5 against 17.0% for top-k), MMLU (13 LLMs, 5,000 questions turned into 10,000 binary samples; about 0.144 error at k = 4 against about 0.150), IMDB (12 LLMs, 91% to 96% accuracy, mean correlation 0.90, "modest" gains). Models include GPT-4.1, GPT-4o, GPT-5-chat, Qwen3-235b, Kimi-k2, Gemini-2.5-flash, Claude-3.5-haiku (MEDMCQA) and Claude-4.5-haiku/sonnet (IMDB), all via OpenRouter.
  - Budget: a query count k out of m models; "No dollar costs are reported". Repeated samples of one model are not used in the ensembles (temperature settings only repeat whole experiments).
- Relevance to AutoAgentNetwork: **supports** (a published theorem that a shared latent factor leaves a non-zero floor) and **threatens novelty** for any claim that "a floor exists". It **differs**: cross-model ensembles, a Gaussian latent with a single uniform rho, a query-count budget, and a selection algorithm; its rho is a latent correlation, not the correlation of 0/1 error indicators (see entry 1's Pearson versus tetrachoric figures).
- Draft related-work sentence: "Turkmen et al. (2026) model correlated model errors with a one-factor Gaussian copula and prove that the error of an infinite ensemble stays above Phi(Phi^-1(1 - alpha)/sqrt(rho)); the floor is the probability that the shared latent pushes the per-item error probability above one half. We estimate the distribution of per-item difficulty from data instead of assuming it is Gaussian, and ask what moves it."
- Resources: no code or data link and no licence statement in the text I read; models accessed through OpenRouter: **UNVERIFIED (no repository seen)**. The arXiv page shows the arXiv perpetual non-exclusive licence.
- Open questions it raises: does the floor formula describe repeated calls of one model, which the one-factor model covers algebraically?

## 11. Correlated Errors in Large Language Models (ICML 2025)

- Authors: Elliot Kim, Avi Garg, Kenny Peng, Nikhil Garg. v1: 9 June 2025. Venue: "Accepted to ICML 2025" (arXiv comment). Read depth: abstract page plus the first 100,000 of 116,937 HTML characters (the last 16,937 not read this session).

- Draft related-work sentence: "Kim et al. (2025) show across 350+ models that errors are substantially correlated (models agree about 60% of the time when both are wrong on HELM; same-company pairs add 2 to 7 points) and that more accurate model pairs are more correlated; they work with pairwise agreement and note that item difficulty is not modelled. ## So what

Changes suggested for `research/plan.md` (Plan v4) and the paper outline. I did not edit them.

1. **Terms block: stop presenting the selection ceiling as new.** The plan defines "selection ceiling" as coverage@k, the share of items that at least one of k systems gets right. That is one minus the all-wrong rate in 2606.27288 (Prop. 1: any select-one policy is at most 1 minus beta; gain over the single best is at most Pr[best wrong] minus beta), published 25 June 2026. Cite it where the term is defined and state our contribution as what moves the ceiling (depth), what it costs, and what selectors reach of it (selection efficiency eta). The "vote ceiling" is the Gaussian-latent floor of 2602.08003 (Theorem 4.4, equality in Appendix C) with the latent distribution left free **[inference from the proof sketch]**; cite that too.

2. **Atlas A2 and A5: add the held-out all-wrong test.** 2606.27288 says a single-factor model underprices the all-wrong tail by about 2.5 times on open-ended math, and its Prop. 3 says beta is not identified by pairwise statistics. Atlas A2 (in-sample, as its heading says) shows the copula column missing the observed tail at k = 5 in both directions. Copula below observed: SWE-bench Verified (0.1845 against 0.2002), LiveBench coding_completion (0.2650 against 0.2697), AIME 2025 (0.0308 against 0.0367), SMT 2025 (0.0354 against 0.0467), ArXivMath-0626 (0.0790 against 0.0881). Copula above observed: LiveBench LCB_generation (0.3014 against 0.2955), LiveBench typos (0.1667 against 0.1567), Apex shortlist (0.0268 against 0.0226), HMMT Feb 2026 (0.0098 against 0.0058). HMMT Feb 2025 is about equal (0.0347 against 0.0352). So the copula is not uniformly low in our data; the claim to test is the paper's, not ours. L0 and L1 match the tail closely, but they carry a free parameter per item, so in-sample agreement is by construction. The fair test is on held-out items; A5 already scores held-out coverage of k-sets, so add all-wrong rate and a Clopper-Pearson interval for it there. The paper can then say whether the factor model beats the copula and the pairwise-correlation route where 2606.27288 says they fail. This costs $0.

3. **Report which correlation you mean.** 2606.27288 (Appendix F) reports Pearson 0.27 against tetrachoric 0.51 for the same data, and says Pearson of 0/1 indicators biases the correlation downward. Kim et al. (agreement when both wrong), Liu 2607.10139 (0.68 / 0.52 / 0.47) and Turkmen (latent rho) use three different quantities. Any comparison table in the paper needs a column for the definition.

4. **Depth is still the open gap, but scope the claim.** None of the eleven treats reasoning effort as an axis compared with width at matched dollars: 2606.27288 excludes pure thinking variants (Appendix D), 2504.01005 uses a thinking model only as one verifier, and 2403.02419 and 2602.08003 count calls or queries. That supports the plan's AH2 and the Atlas A6 (one model at two reasoning settings) as the centre of novelty. But the plan's own Status says AH2 shows "effort low beats effort off at about 1.2x the dollars, not that depth beats a wider vote". The paper's abstract and related work must carry that scoping. Add 2604.02460 (equal thinking tokens) as the nearest agent-system comparison; it was abstract-only in `prior_work.md`, so open it before citing.

5. **AH4 and the verification claim: cite 2504.01005 and keep the comparator.** It is the nearest matched-compute width-versus-verify study and finds SC ahead at practical budgets but GenRM ahead at 128 to 256 times the compute and with a thinking-model verifier (4 times to match). Our result (the Sol judge beats the best cheap pool arm by 13.3 points on BBEH but sits 8.3 below one Sol low call at 1.6 times the dollars, from the plan's Status) has a different comparator, the judge's own answer, which none of the eleven runs. State it as "at matched measured dollars on these items", not as "verification loses".

6. **Label-free selection (Dawid-Skene, agreement).** 2511.07396 (agreement with the strongest model; regret is measured against it), 2607.25018 (self-consistency frequency; needs labels to calibrate; hard queries deferred are ones the strong model also fails) and 2603.06612 (agreement amplifies shared errors; no verifier experiment) all point to the plan's observation that Dawid-Skene put Sonnet at 0.79 against 0.45 true. Cite them as the cascade and polling baselines, and Said and Villaneau (90.7% against 71.0%) as independent evidence of the same inflation.

7. **Cost.** Of the eleven, only 2511.07396 prices in dollars (per-token prices from provider pages, April to May 2025) and 2606.27288 reports spend; neither models cache reads, and 2504.01005, 2403.02419, 2602.08003 and 2607.25018 count FLOPs, calls, queries or a fixed ratio. I found no cache-aware pricing in these eleven. That keeps "arms matched on measured dollars" as a real differentiator, but `prior_work.md` already warns that such a negative holds only for the pages opened.

8. **Method overlap with 2604.00594.** A Rasch model of ability minus difficulty on SWE-bench Verified is published. In the paper, present the factor model as an extension (failure slope, family shocks, call luck) used for composition (AH3), not as the first latent-ability model of agent benchmarks. Their additive LLM-plus-scaffold ability and untested one-dimensionality are things the Atlas can check directly (173 systems on SWE-bench Verified against their 112 combinations; compare item difficulties).

9. **Answer format moves the difficulty axis.** 2606.27288's GPQA result (all-wrong rate 0.127 free-response against about 0 multiple choice; mean accuracy 0.66 to 0.51) means the tasks chosen for the API anchor (BBEH free-form, arXivMath) are the regime where co-failure is large.
