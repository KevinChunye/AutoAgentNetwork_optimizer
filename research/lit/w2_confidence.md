## 1. KalshiBench

### Do Large Language Models Know What They Don't Know? Evaluating Epistemic Calibration via Prediction Markets (arXiv preprint, v1, 17 Dec 2025)
- Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.16030 ; PDF https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.16030 ; dataset https://huggingface.co/datasets/2084Collective/kalshibench-v2 . The arXiv abstract page titles it "KalshiBench: A New Benchmark for Evaluating Epistemic Calibration via Prediction Markets"; the PDF title is the one above. Author: Lukas Nel (Lotus AI per the PDF title page). One version only; arXiv licence CC BY 4.0. The PDF footer carries a "38th Conference on NeurIPS 2024" template line; I found no evidence of a venue.
- What it does: 300 Kalshi yes/no questions (seed 42, from 1,531), all resolving after 1 Oct 2025 (evaluation sample spans 2025-10 to 2025-11, 46 days), five models asked for an answer plus a 0-100 "confidence that the answer is 'yes'". Evaluation sample yes rate 40.0% (Table 1), so the base-rate Brier is 0.4 x 0.6 = 0.24 (my arithmetic).
- Protocol facts that matter for us (Section 4.2): temperature 0.7 (1.0 for GPT-5.2-XHigh); the system prompt ends "Be calibrated: if you're 70% confident, you should be correct about 70% of the time on similar questions." The paper itself says this makes the miscalibration "a failure to follow instructions". The prompt contains a numeric example (70%), which our rule 6 forbids. The protocol mentions neither a market price nor search for the models.
- Key numbers (page images; Table 4, Section 5.1):

| Model | Acc | Brier | BSS | ECE | MCE |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Claude Opus 4.5 | 69.3 | 0.227 | 0.057 | 0.120 | 0.246 |
| Kimi-K2 | 67.1 | 0.347 | -0.446 | 0.298 | 0.570 |
| Qwen3-235B | 65.7 | 0.346 | -0.437 | 0.297 | 0.479 |
| GPT-5.2-XHigh | 65.3 | 0.433 | -0.799 | 0.395 | 0.622 |
| DeepSeek-V3.2 | 64.3 | 0.339 | -0.407 | 0.284 | 0.630 |

- Quotes, Section 5.1: "Key Finding 1: Systematic Overconfidence. All models exhibit substantial calibration errors, with ECE ranging from 0.120 to 0.395. Even the best-calibrated model (Claude Opus 4.5) shows a 12-percentage-point average gap between confidence and accuracy." "Key Finding 2 ... Only Claude Opus 4.5 achieves a positive Brier Skill Score (0.057) ... All other models have negative BSS, meaning their probability estimates are worse than uninformed guessing."
- Table 5 (confidence analysis): average stated confidence 73.7% (DeepSeek, Claude 73.8%) to 81.7% (Qwen); confidence on wrong predictions 69.2% to 80.4% (Claude 71.0%). Section 5.2: "Models maintain 69-80% confidence even on incorrect predictions".
- **High-confidence accuracy (Table 7, Section 5.3), the number you asked for:**

| Model | Avg stated conf (90-100% bin) | Actual accuracy | Gap | n |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Claude Opus 4.5 | 94.6% | 70.0% | +24.6 | 20 |
| DeepSeek-V3.2 | 93.7% | 30.8% | +62.9 | 39 |
| GPT-5.2-XHigh | 95.9% | 33.7% | +62.2 | 104 |
| Qwen3-235B | 94.1% | 46.2% | +47.9 | 39 |
| Kimi-K2 | 94.8% | 37.7% | +57.1 | 61 |

  Section 5.3 on Claude: "even Claude becomes overconfident at high confidence levels: at 90%+ confidence (20 predictions), accuracy is only 70%, yielding a +24.6% gap." On GPT-5.2-XHigh: "only 1 prediction below 50%", "104 predictions (35% of total) in the 90-100% bin where accuracy is merely 33.7%". Section 6.3: "When models express 90%+ confidence, expect 20-30% error rates, not <10%."
- **What "accuracy" means in Table 6/7 (my reading, from the low bins):** the confidence is P(yes), and "Acc" is the share of questions in that bin that resolved yes. Evidence: Claude's 0.0-0.1 bin has stated .054 and "Acc" .194 (n = 36), which only makes sense as a yes-rate. So miscalibration is overconfidence at both ends, not only a yes-bias. Claude Opus 4.5 reliability, Table 6: bin 0.0-0.1 stated .054 vs .194 (n 36); 0.1-0.2 .151 vs .188 (32); 0.8-0.9 .854 vs .688 (16); 0.9-1.0 .946 vs .700 (20). Qwen's 0.0-0.1 bin is .039 vs .356 (n = 73); Kimi's is .047 vs .263 (n = 38).
- **False-conviction rate from Table 6, both tails (my arithmetic, not in the paper):** share of predictions at 0.9 or higher, or 0.1 or lower, that lost: Claude Opus 4.5 about 13 of 56 (23%); Qwen 47 of 112 (42%); Kimi 48 of 99 (48%); DeepSeek 29 of 49 (59%); GPT-5.2-XHigh 69 of 105 (66%). A calibrated model loses at most 10% by definition.
- **Internal inconsistency to know about:** Table 5's OCR@90 (share of errors among predictions above 90% confidence) is 14.7% to 32.4% and Section 5.2 says "models are wrong 15-32% of the time" at 90%+. Tables 6 and 7 give 30.8% to 70.0% accuracy in the top bin, i.e. 30% to 69% wrong, with counts. They cannot both hold. I cite Tables 6 and 7 because they carry counts; I could not reconcile them. Also the text says GPT-5.2-XHigh used "26x more output tokens (~2M vs ~138K for Claude)" while Table 9 lists 2.07M vs 224K tokens (9.2x).
- **Reasoning:** Key Finding 3: "Counterintuitively, GPT-5.2-XHigh (with extended reasoning) shows the worst calibration (ECE=0.395, BSS=-0.799)". This is a cross-model comparison with no with/without-reasoning ablation, and Qwen3-235B-Thinking and Kimi-K2, also labelled reasoning-enhanced, sit at 0.297 and 0.298, close to non-reasoning DeepSeek-V3.2 at 0.284. So the evidence says "reasoning did not rescue calibration", not "reasoning hurts". The paper's mechanisms in Section 6.2 ("Confirmation Bias in Extended Reasoning") are hypotheses.
- **Market-price baseline: none.** The paper compares against the 40% base rate only. It cites human superforecaster Brier 0.15-0.20 and "ECE ≈ 0.03-0.05" (Section 6.4) with no source for the ECE figure. The HF dataset has `market_probability` null (see `research/lit/v3_sources.md`).
- Direction of miscalibration in one line: overconfident in every model, at both ends for the four that gave low-confidence answers; also, for GPT-5.2-XHigh, a P(yes) of 0.9 or more resolved yes 33.7% of the time, below the 40.0% base rate, so its high-conviction calls carried no information.
- **Section 7 invites our design:** "Self-reported confidence (0-100) may not reflect internal probability estimates. Alternative elicitation methods (betting, proper scoring rule incentives) might yield different results." W2 elicits through stakes.
- Relevance to AutoAgentNetwork: **supports** (a prior that stated confidence overshoots, with exact tail numbers); **baseline** (ECE 0.120 for the best Claude model; false-conviction rates above); **dataset** rung 4 fails as already logged. It does **not** ground any Haiku or Sonnet number.
- Resources: HF dataset (no licence on the card, one `train` split of 1,531 rows; sha e70c1077, last modified 2025-12-16). `github.com/2084collective/kalshibench` returned 404 on 2026-10-20.
- Open questions: How many of the 300 events had a favourite far from 50% (the paper gives no price)? Was Opus 4.5 run with thinking on (the prompt asks for a `<think>` block; the paper does not say)? Why do Table 5 and Table 7 disagree?

## 2. "Confidence Without Competence" (Kevin Wang)

### Confidence Without Competence: How Naming an Asset Inflates Language-Model Conviction in Financial Forecasts (SSRN preprint, 2026)
- Link: https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6896281 (DOI 10.2139/ssrn.6896281). The SSRN page returned HTTP 403 on three URL forms, so **I could not open the paper. The abstract was read in the Crossref record** https://api.crossref.org/works/10.2139/ssrn.6896281 (author "Haochuan Wang", ORCID 0009-0002-0182-4370, Crossref record created 2026-07-29), fetched twice with the same text. Kevin's research page https://kevinchunye.github.io/kevinhcw.github.io/research/ lists it as "2026 Preprint" under "Agentic Systems & AI Safety", title and SSRN link only, with no summary. His GitHub profile is "Kevin Haochuan Wang (KevinChunye)", so I take the authorship as his.
- **Not to be confused with** arXiv 2604.09444 "Confidence Without Competence in AI-Assisted Knowledge Work" (Eleftheriou et al., CHIWORK '26), a student-learning study with the same title (search snippet; not opened; not Kevin's).
- What it does (abstract): hourly price-direction prediction ("finegrained enough that memorized training data is not a real concern"), market data held fixed, and one thing changed: "whether the model is told which stock it is looking at". Then it measures "how confident it sounds and how often it is actually right".
- Key numbers (quoted from the abstract; **there are no numbers in it**): "Across six models and six stocks, naming the stock makes the model more confident but no more accurate: its accuracy stays at the coin-flip level an efficient market implies, so the added confidence is unearned. The one exception is the largest model, whose extra confidence comes with a small but real accuracy gain." And: "that confidence reflects how familiar the name is, not how good the forecast is".
- So, to your four questions:
  - Manipulation: naming the asset (telling the model which stock) versus not telling it. It is **not** a vivid story and not a named person or source.
  - Models: "six models", "six stocks". Names UNVERIFIED.
  - Effect size on stated confidence: UNVERIFIED. The metric ("how confident it sounds") may be verbal tone or a stated number; the abstract does not say.
  - Accuracy: near coin-flip in both arms for all but the largest model, which gained "a small but real" amount (size UNVERIFIED).
- What I searched, so you do not repeat it: five web queries on the title, the subtitle and the phrase "naming the stock" (only the Eleftheriou paper and unrelated forecasting papers came back); GitHub profile, both repository pages (45 repositories, none on this) and the profile README; the personal site home and `/research/`; autoxiv.com (not listed); the Google Scholar profile 7RsnZOcAAAAJ (six items, not listed); SSRN (403, three forms); Crossref (abstract found); arXiv API (no match); Semantic Scholar (404, then 429); archive.org (blocked by the fetch tool). No arXiv version found.
- Relevance to AutoAgentNetwork: **supports** the sign of a salience effect on conviction; **threatens nothing**. But it is a different manipulation from W2-A's. Naming a real ticker works through a channel W2-A does not have (prior familiarity, per the abstract's own explanation), while a vivid story about an invented event has no prior to be familiar with. A positive W2-A result is therefore an extension, a null would not contradict the paper, and "replicates the naming effect" in program.md overstates what can be learned. A cleaner design separates name from vividness (name only, vivid detail without a name, both).
- Resources: none found (no code or data link in the abstract or on Kevin's pages).
- Open questions: Which six models (is a Haiku among them)? Effect in what units? What did the largest model gain in accuracy? Is confidence verbal or numeric? Ask Kevin for the results table; he has the numbers and I could not get them.
- Side note, same author, abstract only (summary): "Advertising to Agents: Measuring Description-Framing Bias in LLM Tool Selection" (https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23916) reports that stacked praise raised a tool's pick rate by about 43 percentage points in a preregistered study with two OpenAI models. It shows his framing effects can be large in selection tasks; it says nothing about naming or forecasts.

## 3. The two "Confidence and sizing" sources in program.md

### Ordinal Gates, Cardinal Bets: Matching LLM Confidence to the Financial Decision Operator (arXiv preprint 2609.00187, v2 2 Sep 2026)
- Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.00187 (program.md links the alphaXiv copy). Authors Rayansh Singh, Sara Rezaeimanesh. Abstract read on the arXiv page; Sections 3-6 through the fetch tool's summary of https://arxiv.org/html/2609.00187v2.
- What it does: turns verbalized LLM confidence (0-100) into equity positions for nine open-weight models (Qwen2.5-7B/14B/32B, Gemma-2-9B, Gemma-3-27B, Mistral-7B/24B, Phi-4, FinLLaMa-8B) on FactSet news for Nasdaq-100 names; maps and scales are fitted on 2021 and judged on 2022-2023 (31,888 ticker-days, summary).
- Key numbers (abstract, quoted): "Monotone recalibration cannot change a coverage-matched rank-based gate, whereas position sizing consumes score magnitude"; "scale transfer reduces certainty-equivalent return (CER) in 8/9 models and produces large risk-target errors"; "Matching each map with its fitted scale improves ensemble CER by 9.2 percentage points per year under frozen-scale control (p<0.001)", "+5.5pp/yr" without the largest contributor, and "+1.6pp/yr" under an identical adaptive-volatility controller.
- Summary-only details: sizing is s = D x max(0, 2g(C) - 1) with one scalar scale fitted to a 5% volatility target, clipped at 10% per name and a 100% gross cap; the base classifier is near chance ("1-session accuracy 50.3-51.8%, at or below the 53.8% base rate"); saturated logit confidence gives ECE about 0.47 and temperature scaling brings it to 0.03-0.04; ECE of the verbalized confidence used for sizing is not reported. The summary also flagged that Section 6.2's ensemble returns (3.9% and 12.9%) differ from Table 1 (2.7 and 12.0).
- **On your question:** no Kelly or fractional Kelly is implemented (Kelly appears only in Related Work), and there is no overbetting or leverage finding. Gross exposure averaged 85.2% and the caps "were never binding" (summary). No Claude model.
- Relevance to AutoAgentNetwork: **supports** the W2 premise that confidence has no value until priced through a sizing rule, and that map and scale must be judged together; this bears on S3 (mechanical half-Kelly uses the size of p, which a rank-based gate would not). **Threatens novelty: low** (equities news, open-weight models, no known truth).
- Open questions: does the "scale must be refit when the confidence map changes" result matter for W2, where S3 uses a fixed half-Kelly with no fitted scale?

### Preference Optimization Drives Monoculture in LLM Prediction Markets (arXiv preprint 2606.26583, v1 25 Jun 2026)
- Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.26583 . Authors James Begin et al. Abstract read on the arXiv page; details through the fetch tool's summary of https://arxiv.org/html/2606.26583v1.
- What it does: ten LLM agents trade a binary LMSR market (b = 100) on TruthfulQA pairs; DPO-tuned agents share errors.
- Key numbers (abstract, quoted): "pairwise error correlations of rho = 0.70 and reducing ten agents to the effective forecasting power of ~1.4 independent forecasters", "the 10-agent market (67.6%) fails to match a single standalone agent (70.2%)", "cross-model diversity achieves the largest correlation reduction (rho from 0.68 to 0.40)".
- **On your question:** it does use a betting rule, but not Kelly. Summary of Section 3: agents spend "min(c-p, 0.95) x wealth" where c is stated confidence and p the current price; the paper calls it "a heuristic, not an LMSR-optimal strategy", and Appendix E calls it a "fixed Kelly-like rule". By my arithmetic, Kelly for a $1 contract bought at price p with belief c is (c-p)/(1-p), so this rule is Kelly times (1-p): half-Kelly at p = 0.5, always at or below Kelly at the stated c. Overconfidence evidence, Appendix E (summary): "Agents cluster at 0.9-1.0 confidence regardless of empirical accuracy" and "Because bet size scales with confidence, overconfident agents make aggressively large bets even at high prices." Only accuracy is reported (no Brier, no ECE). Single 8B-class models (Llama 3.1 8B primary), no Claude.
- Relevance to AutoAgentNetwork: **supports** the committee-structure question (S4): a same-model committee of 6 is likely to behave like about 1.3 independent voters (equicorrelated formula N/(1 + (N-1) rho) with rho = 0.70 gives 1.37 for N = 10, matching the paper's 1.4, and 1.33 for N = 6; my arithmetic). Log-odds pooling of correlated overconfident agents will not average the overconfidence away; summing log-odds would multiply it. The plan should say which pooling it means. **Threatens novelty: low.**
- Open questions: does cross-vendor diversity (the OpenAI arm in W2) move rho as much as 0.68 to 0.40 for our prompts?

## 4. Published measurements of LLM bet sizing against Kelly

**Result: I found no paper that reports an overbet rate or a fraction-of-Kelly for LLM bets, per bet, against a computed Kelly stake.** Searches (about eight, including "LLM Kelly overbet", "biased coin LLM", "fraction of Kelly language model") returned only the following, in decreasing order of closeness. Not an exhaustive review.

- **Kelly yardstick (primary, page images).** MacLean, Thorp and Ziemba, "Good and bad properties of the Kelly criterion" (2010), https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~aldous/157/Papers/Good_Bad_Kelly.pdf . p. 1: "the optimal Kelly wager is the edge (expected return) divided by the odds"; p. 3: "the growth rate becomes zero plus the risk free rate when one bets exactly twice the Kelly wager"; "As you exceed the Kelly bets more and more, risk increases and long term growth falls, eventually becoming more and more negative." p. 1 and Table 1 on p. 2 (citing Chopra and Ziemba 1993): "Errors in means versus errors in variances were about 20:2:1 in importance" (means : variances : covariances), so a biased p is the dominant input error for a Kelly sizer. My arithmetic check, p = 0.6 at even odds (Kelly 0.2): growth per bet is +0.0201 at f = 0.2 and -0.0024 at f = 0.4.
- **KellyBench** (arXiv 2604.27865, summary). Five models bet a 2023/24 Premier League season over five seeds each. Table 2 average ROI: GPT-5.4 -7.9%, Claude Opus 4.6 -11.2%, GLM-5 -51.6%, Gemini 3.1 Pro -66.0%, Kimi K2.5 -89.6%; "Only 3 out of 25 seeds achieved a positive return on investment"; ruin in three models. Section 4.1.1: "Nearly every model discussed Kelly staking in its chain of thought", but 9 of 25 seeds used no principled stake sizing at execution time and 7 of 25 wrote Kelly code that was never called (Table 4). Appendix A: Opus 4.6 used "fractional Kelly" and, in its best seed, "quarter-Kelly and an 8% bankroll cap"; GPT-5.4 used flat 4-8% stakes; Gemini 3.1 Pro placed "thirteen simultaneous wagers on matchday 2 totalling 38% of its bankroll"; Kimi K2.5 had a call that put 98% of the bankroll on one match. **The paper never compares each stake with a computed Kelly stake** (summary of the whole text), so it gives no overbet rate. Relevance: **baseline/threat**: evidence that models know Kelly but often do not apply it, which is the case for mechanical sizing (S3). It is also the nearest prior art for W2; its edge is estimated by the agent, so belief error and sizing error are not separated.
- **WC2026-Agents** (arXiv 2607.17765, PDF pages read). Four consumer chat models (Claude is listed as Claude Opus 4.8 in the references, plus ChatGPT, Gemini, Grok) forecast all 104 World Cup 2026 matches through consumer interfaces with tools, with a virtual $100 stake. Section 6.6: "Grok's stake tracks its confidence most tightly (Pearson r=0.85) ... Gemini and ChatGPT also stake up with confidence (r=0.54 and 0.53), but Gemini's mean stake ($84 of a $100 budget) is a near-max-bet habit that inflates variance ... Claude is the reverse: its stake is negatively related to confidence (r= -0.20)". ROI at real odds (Figure 3): Claude -18.1%, ChatGPT +8.0%, Gemini +3.7%, Grok +10.3%; a flat $100 on the market favourite returned +$1,041. Agent probabilities against the market-implied probability: r = 0.97 to 0.99 (Figure 2). **Calibration runs the other way here:** "All four agents lie above the reliability diagonal: they are under-confident on their top pick"; ECE Gemini 0.068, Grok 0.097, ChatGPT 0.111, Claude 0.116 (Section 6.3). No Kelly comparison (it is offered as future work). Relevance: **baseline**, and a **counter-example** to "LLMs are always overconfident" and to "stakes track conviction" (Claude's did not).
- **Going All-In on LLM Accuracy** (Todasco, arXiv 2512.05998, PDF read). Predictors (Grok 4, Mistral Large 2, Qwen 3 Max; no Claude) bet 1 to 100,000 "LLMCoin" from a 1,000,000 bankroll at even odds on whether other models answer maths questions correctly. Table 7: stakes of 1-1,000 were right 73.9% (n = 871), 1,001-10,000 right 76.0% (2,249), 10,001-40,000 right 89.1% (2,110), 40,000 or more right 98.8% (170). Average ROI 569% and no predictor lost money in any round. Section 3.4: "Qwen 3 Max Thinking behaved most like a disciplined Kelly-style bettor (sizing bets proportionally to edge)", a qualitative label with no Kelly calculation. Each stake is capped at 10% of the starting bankroll and the sum of stakes in a round may not exceed the bankroll, which limits how far overbetting can go, and the paper computes no Kelly stake. Relevance: **supports** "stake size carries conviction information" in a setting with known truth, but it cannot show overbetting.
- **Can Large Language Models Develop Gambling Addiction?** (arXiv 2509.22818, summary). Slot machine with 30% win rate, 3x payout (expected value -10%), $100 start; models include Claude-3.5-Haiku. Table 4 bankruptcy rate, fixed $10 bet versus variable $5-$100 bet: Claude 3.5-Haiku 0.00% vs 20.50%; GPT-4o-mini 0.00% vs 21.31%; Gemini-2.5-Flash 3.12% vs 48.06%; GPT-4.1-mini 0.00% vs 6.31%. Relevance: a negative-edge game, no Kelly, and an old Haiku, but it measures the same hazard: letting the model pick the stake raises ruin. It supports the S1-versus-S3 contrast (free sizing versus mechanical sizing).
- **Prophet Arena** (arXiv 2510.17638, summary) sizes every bet as a fixed $1 on the side with an edge; **Raven-Agent** (arXiv 2607.03015, summary) applies "one quarter of the Kelly bet" to every candidate by design. Neither measures LLM-chosen sizes.
- **Human baseline for scale (secondary, Wikipedia on Haghani and Dewey):** 61 participants betting on a 60%-heads coin (Kelly 20% of bankroll): "28% of the participants went bust, and the average payout was just $91"; "18 of the 61 participants bet everything on one toss".

## 5. Salience and vividness effects on LLM probability judgments

**Result: I found no measurement of an irrelevant vivid story or anecdote moving an LLM's probability. The usable numbers are for one-sentence informative context, and for confidence shifts under personas.**

### OptimismBench: Forecasting Bias and the Alignment Effect in Language Model Judgment (arXiv preprint 2607.26981, v1 29 Jul 2026)
- Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.26981 ; PDF read pp. 1-8. Authors Seonglae Cho, Adriano Koshiyama.
- What it does: for 60 invented, third-person scenarios, asks for P(success) and P(failure) separately; "Skew" = P(success) - (100 - P(failure)), in points, positive meaning optimistic (Eq. 1). 16 models, 5 to 10 runs per item at temperature 0.7 (Opus 4.6, Gemini Pro 3.1 and DeepSeek-V3.2 at 1.0).
- **Claude numbers (Table 1, Track B):** Haiku 4.5 **+6.1** (sigma 11.7), Opus 4.6 **-5.1**, Sonnet 4.6 **-7.7**. Quote: "Within Anthropic the lightweight Haiku 4.5 is optimistic (+6.1) while the larger Opus and Sonnet are pessimistic". The abstract: "fourteen are optimistic; pessimism appears only in Anthropic's frontier tier" (the paper calls Sonnet 4.6 both "balanced" and "frontier").
- **Explicit numbers remove the bias (Section 4.1):** "On Track A items where the base rate is explicitly stated in the scenario, all models report the stated value, yielding a directional offset of 0.00." Bias appears "when the model constructs a probability, not when it retrieves it."
- **Narrative sentence (Section 3.3 and 4.4):** one appended sentence, for example "The founder has 10 years of industry experience" versus "The founder has no prior startup experience". "Appending a single sentence of positive or negative context shifts estimates by +13-15 pp (positive narrative) and -7-10 pp (negative), yielding total susceptibility of +20.5 (GPT-5.4) and +25.8 (Claude Sonnet). Yet the direction of bias is preserved". Only GPT-5.4 and Claude Sonnet were tested. These sentences carry directional information, so this is an upper bound for a no-information story.
- Relevance: **baseline** for the size of a context effect on Claude, and for signed bias by arm (Haiku and Sonnet lean opposite ways). Also supports reporting a signed bias, not only ECE.
- Open questions: does a story with no directional content move estimates at all?

### Other sources, weaker
- **Do Language Models Mirror Human Confidence?** (ACL 2025 Findings, arXiv 2506.00582; pp. 4-8 read). Abstract: "when prompted to answer based on different personas ... the models will respond with stereotypically biased confidence estimations even though their underlying answer accuracy remains the same." Section 5.2: "All models consistently show pronounced overplacement for expert roles" (overplacement is the model's confidence about what a persona would score, not its confidence in its own answer), and "actual model accuracy remains relatively consistent across different personas". Section 8: "LLMs can be easily manipulated by expert personas, leading to inflated confidence scores." Models: Llama-3-70B-instruct, Claude-3-Sonnet, GPT-4o. Effect sizes are only in bar charts and heat maps (Figures 4 and 5), no table. This is the closest independent support for "confidence moves, accuracy does not" when a person or role is named.
- **Evaluating LLMs on Real-World Forecasting Against Expert Forecasters** (Lu, arXiv 2507.04562, summary). The narrative prompt (a script between Nate Silver and Philip Tetlock) lowered accuracy: median Brier Claude-3.6-Sonnet 0.1810 direct to 0.2345 narrative, Claude-3.5-Sonnet 0.1947 to 0.2415, o3 0.1352 to 0.1985. Section 5.5: with narrative prompts "both Claude Sonnet models are extremely underconfident". A story frame can therefore move Claude toward less conviction, so the sign of a salience effect is not guaranteed. Old models, and the manipulation is a prompt style, not a story inside the question.
- **CogBias** (arXiv 2604.01366, summary): base-rate neglect and "Information Processing & Salience" are named bias families, but the paper reports no vividness-specific number for any model (three non-Claude models). A search snippet quoted a "90% against 10% Bayesian" example; I could not confirm it is a result, so do not cite it.
- Not opened (snippets only): look-ahead and name-masking papers for stock forecasts (e.g. arXiv 2309.17322); none measured confidence under named versus masked entities.

## 6. Haiku-class calibration numbers found elsewhere (all different tasks)

- **The Dunning-Kruger Effect in Large Language Models** (arXiv 2603.09985; PDF pp. 1-5 read; the page stamp says 12 Feb 2026). Claude Haiku 4.5, Gemini 2.5 Pro and Flash, Kimi K2 on MMLU, TriviaQA, ARC, HellaSwag (1,500 questions each), 0-100 confidence, **extended thinking on, temperature 0**. Table 1: Haiku 4.5 accuracy 75.4% [74.3, 76.5], mean confidence 86.0, confidence std 41.0, **ECE 0.122**, best of four (Gemini 2.5 Pro 0.185, Flash 0.272, Kimi K2 0.726). Per dataset (summary): TriviaQA ECE 0.43 at 45.9% accuracy; ARC 0.03. Caveat: a standard deviation of 41 on a 0-100 scale with a mean of 86 is implausible for clean data and suggests a parsing or scale problem; the ECE may be unreliable. Mean confidence minus accuracy is +10.6 points (my arithmetic): overconfident.
- **LLM-as-a-Prophet: Prophet Arena** (arXiv 2510.17638, summary). 1,367 resolved Kalshi events (72,136 markets, cutoff 11 Oct 2025), each model sees news and **the current market prices**. Table 2 ECE: Claude Sonnet 4 (reasoning) **0.041**, GPT-5 0.042, Grok-4 0.043, Llama-4-Scout 0.060, Gemini 2.5 Flash 0.067, Market baseline 0.069; text: "all the selected LLMs demonstrate better calibration than the market baseline". Brier for Sonnet 4: 0.194. Section 4.2.3: "LLMs consistently output more conservative probabilities". This is the lower bound on ECE when the price is visible; the same models are not tested without it.
- **What LLM Forecasters Know but Don't Say** (arXiv 2607.08046, summary): an 8B Qwen-derived forecaster's verbalized confidence has ECE 0.093 and "rollouts stated at 0.75 resolve correct only 44% of the time" (overconfident above 0.5). No Claude forecaster.
- None of the sources reports Haiku 5.5 or Sonnet 5.5.

## What this predicts for W2-A

All three are analogies. None of the studies uses synthetic clue-and-prior events, and none tests Haiku 5.5 or Sonnet 5.5. Use them as ranges, not point predictions.

**1. ECE of the stated p for a Haiku-class agent.**
- Anchors: Opus 4.5 on Kalshi with no price and no search, ECE **0.120** (KalshiBench Table 4); the other four models **0.284 to 0.395**; Haiku 4.5 on knowledge QA **0.122** (Dunning-Kruger paper, Table 1, with a data-quality doubt); Claude Sonnet 4 with price and news visible **0.041** (Prophet Arena Table 2); Claude Opus 4.8 on football **0.116** but under-confident (WC2026-Agents, Section 6.3).
- Suggested preregistration: central range **0.08 to 0.20** for the Haiku arm; 80% interval **0.04 to 0.30**; the Sonnet arm no worse than the Haiku arm. Reaching the low end requires p to collapse onto the visible price q (WC2026-Agents Figure 2: r = 0.97 to 0.99), which would show up as belief error equal to q's error. This range is my judgement from the anchors above.
- Direction: **overconfident, too extreme at both ends** (KalshiBench Table 6: Opus stated .054 against a .194 yes-rate in the lowest bin and .946 against .700 in the highest). A competing result is under-confidence on a top pick (WC2026-Agents), so preregister the direction as a hypothesis, not a certainty. Signed lean: Haiku 4.5 optimistic **+6.1** points and Sonnet 4.6 pessimistic **-7.7** (OptimismBench Table 1), so the two arms may lean opposite ways on P(yes). Where the prior is stated as a number the offset was 0.00 (OptimismBench Section 4.1), so expect a smaller lean in W2-A than the vignette numbers.
- False-conviction rate (bets at 90% or more that lose): KalshiBench Opus 4.5 **30%** on the yes side (6 of 20, Table 7) and about **23%** over both tails (my arithmetic from Table 6), against a calibrated 10% or less; other models 42% to 66%. Preregister 20% to 35% for Claude-class and treat a rate under 10% as the surprise.

**2. Sizing error: direction depends on whether the agent sizes itself.**
- Against Kelly at the true probability, the product of belief error and sizing rule decides. Illustration at an assumed q = 0.5 using Opus 4.5's top bin (stated 0.946, actual yes-rate 0.700; my arithmetic): Kelly at the stated p is 0.892 of bankroll and at the true p is 0.400, a 2.2x overbet; log growth per bet is **-0.221** for the stated-p full Kelly, **+0.081** for half-Kelly at the stated p (stake 0.446) and **+0.082** at the true Kelly. MacLean et al. (p. 3): growth is zero at exactly twice Kelly. For the four non-Claude models' top bins (actual 31% to 46%, below q = 0.5) the true Kelly is zero, so every stake is an overbet. So **S3 half-Kelly should survive Opus-sized overconfidence but not GPT-5.2-XHigh-sized overconfidence**, and the sizing error will concentrate in bets whose true edge is zero or negative (wrong-sign bets), not in oversizing real edges.
- Self-chosen stakes (S1): the evidence leans toward fractional sizing for Claude, not overbetting: KellyBench Appendix A (Opus 4.6 "fractional Kelly", "quarter-Kelly and an 8% bankroll cap"), WC2026-Agents (Claude "stakes least"; stake against confidence r = -0.20). Contrary: Gemini's near-max stakes ($84 of $100 mean) and 38% of bankroll on one matchday (KellyBench), and "gambling" bankruptcy 20.5% for Claude-3.5-Haiku when it chose its own stake against 0.00% at a fixed stake (Table 4). So preregister the **overbet rate against Kelly at the true p as mostly from wrong-sign or no-edge bets, with a fat right tail of oversized stakes**, and the mean stake against Kelly at the agent's own p below 1. No published number gives a fraction-of-Kelly for Claude; I would not preregister a point value.
- Amplification (my derivation from f* = (p - q)/(1 - q)): a shift of d in p moves the Kelly stake by d/(1 - q), so 2d at q = 0.5 and 5d at q = 0.8. A small effect on p becomes a larger effect on f in S3.

**3. Salience effect.**
- Sign: **positive on stated conviction, no change in accuracy** (Wang's abstract: "more confident but no more accurate"; persona paper: confidence shifts, "accuracy remains relatively consistent"). Counter-evidence for the sign: Lu 2025 narrative prompts left Claude Sonnet "extremely underconfident".
- Size: **no published number for a non-informative story.** Upper bound: one informative sentence moves Claude Sonnet and GPT-5.4 by +13 to +15 points (positive) and -7 to -10 (negative) (OptimismBench Section 4.4). Because the W2-A story carries no information by construction, preregister a shift of **0 to +5 points in stated p or in confidence on the chosen side, with a 15-point ceiling** (my judgement; the persona paper has no table to scale from). Plan on the decision rule being underpowered if the true shift is under about 3 points, since the effect on f is amplified only through S1 or S3.

## So what

`research/plan.md` has no W2 section yet (it mentions W2-A only for budget), so these are changes to the W2 text in `research/v3/program.md` and to the W2-A plan when it is written.

1. **Do not call the salience test a replication.** Wang's naming effect is naming a real ticker (familiarity); W2-A uses a vivid named story on an invented event. Rename the decision line to "tests whether a salience cue inflates conviction (an extension)", and consider a three-cell design (name only, vivid detail only, both) so a positive result says which part works. Ask Kevin for the paper's table so the preregistered effect size is not a guess.
2. **Preregister ranges from sections above, not point values**, and state that none is measured on Haiku 5.5 or Sonnet 5.5: ECE 0.08-0.20 central, overconfident at both tails; false-conviction rate 20-35% against a calibrated 10%; salience shift 0 to +5 points; signed P(yes) lean per arm (Haiku +, Sonnet -).
3. **Elicit p blind to q, then show q for sizing.** Models that see the market price copy it (r = 0.97 to 0.99) and look well calibrated for that reason (Prophet Arena: ECE 0.041 against a market 0.069). With q visible during belief elicitation the "belief error" measures q, not the agent. Two calls per decision on a subsample is enough to measure the gap.
4. **Prompt wording.** KalshiBench's prompt embeds "70%"; the Monoculture and KalshiBench results both show clustering at 0.9 to 1.0. Under rule 6, use placeholders and clip p to [0.01, 0.99] before the Kelly step. Record the confidence histogram, since clustering is the failure.
5. **Split the overbet metric.** Report (a) wrong-sign or no-edge bets (true Kelly = 0, any stake is an overbet) and (b) oversizing of real edges. The Opus illustration shows a 25-point overconfidence costs little under half-Kelly when the sign is right and everything when it is wrong.
6. **Fix the sizing-error definition before S3 is judged.** Half-Kelly at the agent's own p has a built-in 50% gap to full Kelly, so "cuts sizing error by a third" is trivially met against Kelly at own p. Judge S3 against Kelly at the true p, in log growth. Also state whether the committee's "log-odds pooled" means mean or sum: for same-model agents with error correlation near 0.70 the effective size of 6 is about 1.3 (Monoculture rho = 0.70, N_eff about 1.4 for 10), and a sum of log-odds would multiply their shared overconfidence.
7. **Report a signed bias and the stake-confidence correlation per arm**, not only ECE. Claude arms may lean opposite ways (OptimismBench), and Claude's stake was negatively related to its confidence in WC2026-Agents (r = -0.20).
8. **Novelty.** KellyBench and WC2026-Agents are the nearest prior art on LLM staking; neither knows the true probability, so neither can split a loss into belief error and sizing error. That split under known truth is W2-A's contribution. This rests on about eight searches, not an exhaustive review.
9. **W2-B rung 4 stays a logged failure.** KalshiBench has no price and nothing after 2025-11-16 (`research/lit/v3_sources.md`); use it only as the source of the numbers above.
